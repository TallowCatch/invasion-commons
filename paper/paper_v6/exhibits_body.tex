  \caption{How harm connects the agents in each game. (a)~\textsc{Fishery} is a star: six agents draw on one stock, so each agent's catch lowers what all can take next round; the reviewer's single shared cut (dashed ring) also links every agent, in all three games. (b)~\textsc{Forest} is a ring: each agent cuts its own plot, and over-cutting damages the two neighbouring plots ($0.12$ of the excess each). (c)~\textsc{River} is a mixing junction: three agents discharge reagent $X$ and three reagent $Y$; damage $\kappa XY+\lambda(X+Y)$ comes mainly from the two together, so no single discharge looks harmful on its own.}

\begin{figure}[H]

  \caption{\textbf{What the reviewer aims for, and what it knows, decide how well it protects the resource.} Top row, \textsc{Fishery}: (a)~fish stock over one run when the reviewer sees all requests (4 greedy agents; line: middle run, band: 80\% of 64 populations). With only the safety line, the stock is fished down to just above 10; with the MSY target it stays near 70. (b)~Total harvest in six settings (``4g 0.7'' = 4 greedy agents, regrowth 0.7; $^\dagger$ the pilot setting). The MSY target gives more wherever the safety line limits anything (1,394 against 544 in the pilot setting). (c)~Share of runs in which the stock collapsed when the reviewer's model is wrong: 41\% (regrowth overestimated) and 78\% (an unknown tipping point) with the safety line, 0\% with the MSY target. Bottom row, \textsc{Forest}: (d)~risky requests let through and (e)~safe requests blocked, when one part of the reviewer's model is wrong. The direction of the error decides the mistake: thinking regrowth is faster lets 82\% of risky requests through; thinking it slower blocks 42\% of safe ones. The cautious local reviewer blocks most safe requests in every case. (f)~A joint reviewer that learns the model while running lets through 16\% of risky requests early and 2.5\% late. Error bars: 95\% intervals.}
  \label{fig:reviewer}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_audits.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{What makes audits work: memory, an expected fine above the gain, and audits that reach everyone.} (a)~Under-reporters in nine settings (one random audit per step, no fines). Auditing without memory barely reduces harm; remembering each caught lie removes most of it. $^*$\textsc{Fishery}: steps above the MSY limit; \textsc{Forest}: steps below the safety line. (b)~\textsc{Fishery}: the expected fine at which cheaters stop ($e^*$) against the break-even predicted from no-check runs ($g^*$, the gain from one step of cheating). Filled: five settings, predicted before the search (R3). Open: the pilot setting at audit rates from 1/6 to 0.02 and with audits that miss three cheats in four (S3). Every point lies within one grid step (shaded) of the prediction. (c)~Cheaters' gain as the audit rate rises, under five audit rules (fine 6; bands: 95\% intervals). A fine stops cheating from an audit rate of 0.10; memory alone leaves about half the gain; tightening everyone's cut to allow for known cheaters makes cheating pay more than with no audits. (d)~Harvest per honest agent at the same audit rate (1/6) and fine (24): random audits stop cheating; predictable audits (every agent on every sixth step) leave honest agents worse off than no audits (28.8 against 40.9). (e)~Harm left, as a share of the harm with no audits, when one audit per step (with memory) is aimed at random, at the largest report, or, in \textsc{Forest}, at the plot doing worst against prediction. Numbers above the bars: the share of liars ever caught. Aiming at the largest report keeps auditing the same agent, so in \textsc{Fishery} only 25\% of liars are ever caught and harm stays at 97\% of the no-audit level. Error bars: 95\% intervals.}
  \label{fig:audits}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4_llm_agents.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Language-model fishers stop over-taking only when the expected fine exceeds their gain.} \textsc{Fishery}, MSY target, 20 rounds; four of six fishers are language models, audited with probability 1/6 per round; 10 populations per cell. (a)~Share of the models' rounds in which they caught more than their allowance, against the fine $F$ (expected fine $e = F/6$; fines of 12--30~t are from L3, gpt-oss and Nemotron only). gpt-oss and Nemotron over-take in 51--92\% of rounds for every $e$ up to 1.33~t. Over-taking then falls steadily, and is at most 2\% from the first fine above their measured gain from one round of over-taking ($g$ = 4.48 and 4.74~t, dotted lines; shaded: $e > g$ for both). The stopping fine predicted from $g$ before L3 ran ($F = 30$) was exact for Nemotron and one step early for gpt-oss ($F = 24$). Gemma over-takes little at every fine; Mistral Large 3 (an added fourth family, open triangles) never over-takes. (b)~What the two rule-following fishers catch over the 20 rounds: 3--25~t while fines are far below the gain, and 32--37~t once over-taking stops. (c)~The same comparison when the rules leave out the sentence that over-taking is allowed (silent) or say it in other words (reworded): a 36~t fine stops over-taking under every wording. (d)~What an over-take step is (post hoc): gpt-oss and Nemotron mostly catch more than they asked for, while Gemma almost only keeps its request after the reviewer cut it. Error bars: 95\% intervals over populations.}
  \label{fig:llm}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig5_spine.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Each kind of agent stops cheating when the expected fine outweighs the gain it acts on.} Shaded: the expected fine exceeds the gain on the horizontal axis. (a)~Simulated cheaters choose one cheating level for the whole game, so they are scaled by their whole-game net gain $g^*$ (gain over complying, per round of cheating). In five \textsc{Fishery} settings they stop at $e/g^* = 1.0$--$1.2$, as predicted before the search (R3). (b)~Language-model agents decide every round, and are scaled by their one-round gain $g_1$ (extra tonnes in that round), measured with no fine. They stop close to $e/g_1 = 1$: the stopping fine predicted from $g_1$ before L3 ran was exact for Nemotron and one grid step early for gpt-oss. They begin to cut back from about $e/g_1 = 0.4$. Measured by the whole-game gain, they keep cheating far past the point where it pays. Over-taking loses gpt-oss fish over the game ($g^* < 0$), and Nemotron's whole-game break-even (dotted) lies at $e/g_1 = 0.15$. They act on the gain of the round, not of the game. Filled: L2; open: L3. Error bars: 95\% intervals over populations.}
  \label{fig:spine}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/figA1_llm_behaviour.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Appendix, post hoc: how the language-model agents over-take} (L2, gpt-oss and Nemotron; analyses chosen after the results were seen). (a)~After over-taking, the chance of over-taking again in the next round barely depends on whether the agent was caught, with a fine below its gain or with no fine. Audits are independent, so a rational agent should not change; people often comply less right after an audit (the ``bomb-crater'' effect; Mittone 2006; Kastlunger et al.\ 2009). (b)~When they over-take, they mostly take the 6~t maximum: under a flat fine nothing deters taking more once over (no marginal deterrence; Stigler 1970). Error bars: 95\% Wilson intervals over agent-rounds, which are not independent; descriptive only.}
  \label{fig:a1}
\end{figure}
