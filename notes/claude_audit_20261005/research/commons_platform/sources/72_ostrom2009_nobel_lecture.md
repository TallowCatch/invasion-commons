## Verbatim quotes

1. Design philosophy and source of the payoff function:
   > "the initial cPr experiments started with a static, baseline situation that was as simple as could be specified without losing crucial aspects of the appropriation problems facing harvesters in the field. We used a quadratic payoff production function based on Gordon's (1954) classic model."
   (Lower-case "cPr" as extracted from the PDF.)
2. Payoff (E. Ostrom, Gardner and Walker 1994: 110): u_i(x) = we if x_i = 0; u_i(x) = w(e − x_i) + (x_i/Σx_i)F(Σx_i) if x_i > 0, with eight subjects, endowment e, outside return w.
3. Equilibrium versus optimum:
   > "the prediction from noncooperative game theory was that subjects would invest according to the nash equilibrium – 8 tokens each for a total of 64 tokens. subjects could earn considerably more if they re- duced their allocation to a total of 36 tokens in the common-pool resource."
4. The sanctioning treatment:
   > "after subjects played ten rounds of the baseline common-pool resource game, they were told that in the subsequent rounds they would have an opportunity to pay a fee in order to impose a fine on another subject."
5. External regulation with random inspection (Cardenas, Stranlund and Willis 2000, Colombian villagers):
   > "the prob- ability of an inspection was 1/16 per round – a low but realistic probability for monitoring rule conformance in rural areas in developing countries."
   Result: subjects under the imposed rule "increased their withdrawal levels when compared to the outcomes obtained when face-to-face communication was allowed and no rule was imposed" (crowding out).

## Relevance
States explicitly why the CPR lab game looks the way it does (simplest structure that keeps the field problem, Gordon's model) and gives the Nash/optimum numbers. Most important for Ameer: the only CPR experiment here with an external, randomly inspecting regulator used an inspection probability of 1/16 per round, justified as realistic for the field, and found that the imposed rule crowded out cooperation. This is a direct human precedent for an audit-probability choice and a known risk (crowding out) that an overseer study should test. Note: the exact F(·) coefficients are not printed in the lecture; the commonly cited form F(X) = 23X − 0.25X² with w = $0.05 is consistent with the 36/64 figures (optimum X = (23−5)/(2×0.25) = 36; Nash X = 8/9 × 72 = 64) but was not seen in a primary text in this run.
