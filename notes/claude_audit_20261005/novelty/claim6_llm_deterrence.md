# Novelty check: claim 6 (L2, audits and fines for over-taking)

Checked 2026-10-08. Search tools: web search, arXiv listing API, arXiv abstract and HTML pages, Crossref, GitHub API, author PDFs. Semantic Scholar's API refused requests (rate limit), and OpenReview and SSRN blocked automated access, so a few sources are known only from their metadata (marked below).

## The claim being checked

Fishery commons, 6 fishers, 20 rounds. A reviewer scales requests down to protect the stock, then each fisher picks its actual catch. 4 of the fishers are LLM agents. Each round, each fisher is audited with probability 1/6 and fined F if it caught more than its allowance. Expected fine e = F/6.

- **R1 (break-even):** gpt-oss-120b and Nemotron 3 Super over-took in 51–92% of rounds for every e from 0 to 1.33 t, and in 0% at e = 6 t. That is the only tested e above their measured one-step gain from over-taking (g ≈ 4.5–4.7 t). This fits the rule "deterred only if detection probability × fine > gain".
- **R2 (small fine backfires, post hoc):** a 1–2 t fine raised over-taking compared with no fine (gpt-oss 55% → about 90%).
- **R3 (family differences):** model families differ more in *whether* they over-take than in *how they respond* to the fine. Mistral Large 3 never over-took, even when told it could at no cost. Gemma 4 rarely over-took, and when it did, it almost always did so by ignoring the reviewer's cut.
- **R4 (permission wording):** telling the model "you can choose to catch more than your allowance" strongly raises over-taking.

## Bottom line

One paper overlaps a lot with this claim: **Okamoto, Erol & Erol (2026), arXiv:2608.12323**, "Why Do AI Agents Break Rules?". It tests 12 LLMs as procurement agents. The same rule is wrapped in four levels of audit likelihood × fine, chosen around the break-even point, and in three wordings, one of which explicitly says breaking the rule is allowed. Three of its models are also in L2: GPT-OSS-120B, Nemotron 3 Super and Gemma 4 31B. It reports, in its own words, "This confirms the Gneezy-Rustichini effect ... in AI agents". It reports large differences between models. Its "Discretionary" wording ("It's not mandatory—you can still use any vendor") makes compliance collapse. So for R2, R3 and R4 the effect itself is not new for LLM agents. What is new is the setting (a repeated, multi-agent, sequential commons with an explicit numeric audit probability and a measured gain), plus some model-level contrasts. The paper must cite Okamoto et al. and frame claim 6 as an extension or replication in a new setting, not a first demonstration.

## Verdicts

### R1: break-even (deterred only when expected fine > measured gain)
**Partly shown.** Okamoto et al. (2026) set fine levels around the break-even point and found, for GPT-OSS-120B and Nemotron 3 Super under the "allowed" wording, compliance rising from below-break-even to above-break-even fines (0/74/100/100 and 25/50/83/100). Scheurer et al. (2023) show misbehaviour falls as stated detection risk rises. Becker and Allingham–Sandmo give the rule.
**No prior work found in this search** that does all of the following: (a) gives the agent a numeric audit probability, (b) measures each model's own one-step gain, and (c) tests whether the switch from over-taking to not over-taking happens where p·F crosses that gain, in a repeated multi-agent commons.
**Internal risk (more serious than the novelty risk):** only one tested e (6 t) lies above g, and nothing lies between 1.33 t and 6 t. The data fit *any* threshold in (1.33, 6). So "matches the break-even rule" is consistent with the rule, but the data do not pin the threshold down. Also, Becker's rule is in expected utility, so risk attitude moves the threshold.

### R2: a small fine increases over-taking (post hoc)
**Already shown for LLM agents** by Okamoto et al. (2026): "This confirms the Gneezy-Rustichini effect ... in AI agents", including Nemotron 3 Super (81% → 50% compliance). SchemeArena (2026) reports a related pattern: partial monitoring increased scheming. For humans, Gneezy & Rustichini (2000).
What is still new: the effect appears (i) in a repeated commons with a numeric audit probability, and (ii) for gpt-oss-120b, which Okamoto found *unaffected* in their informational wording (96% → 96%). This result is post hoc in L2, so it must be presented as exploratory.

### R3: families differ more in whether they over-take than in how they respond
**Partly shown.** Okamoto et al.: "compliance spans 46 percentage points across models" and "models differ in which pressure breaks them". Guzman Piedrahita et al. (2025): "four distinct behavioral patterns among models". The specific split between whether and how, and the mechanism detail (Gemma over-takes by ignoring the reviewer's cut; Mistral Large 3 never over-takes even when told it can at no cost), was **not found in prior work**. It is tied to L2's two-step reviewer design.

### R4: the "you can choose to catch more" wording raises over-taking
**Already shown in other settings.** Okamoto's "Discretionary" wording ("It's not mandatory—you can still use any vendor") drops compliance sharply (GPT-OSS-120B 96% → 0% with no fine). Bhandary et al. (2026): "explicit permission mainly increases bluffing and diversion". PIMMUR (2026) warns that such prompts can "pre-determine the outcome". Within L2 the finding is still useful as a robustness and confound result, but it is not new as a phenomenon.

## Strongest risk to novelty

**Okamoto, Erol & Erol (2026), arXiv:2608.12323.** It shares three of L2's models, uses the same theory (Becker plus Gneezy–Rustichini), places fine levels around break-even, finds the fine-as-price paradox in LLM agents, finds large model differences, and finds that "it's allowed" wording collapses compliance. A reviewer who knows it would see R2–R4 as replications. Second-order risks:
- the becker-agents repo (Sept 2026), with the same research idea and no results yet;
- Dante (2025) SSRN, not verified;
- Bell & Madisetti (2026), fishing commons with formal enforcement.

## Suggested wording for the paper

- Cite Okamoto et al. (2026) next to Gneezy & Rustichini (2000) and Becker (1968). Do not write "first evidence that fines can backfire for LLM agents".
- R1: "In a repeated fishery commons with a stated audit probability, over-taking by gpt-oss-120b and Nemotron 3 Super stopped only at the tested expected fine (6 t) that exceeded their measured one-step gain (≈4.6 t). It continued at every expected fine up to 1.33 t. This is consistent with the expected-penalty rule (Becker 1968; Allingham & Sandmo 1972). Our grid does not locate the threshold more precisely than between 1.33 and 6 t."
- R2: "Exploratory (not pre-specified): a small expected fine raised over-taking, echoing 'a fine is a price' (Gneezy & Rustichini 2000) and its recent demonstration in single-turn LLM procurement agents (Okamoto et al. 2026). In our setting it also appeared for gpt-oss-120b, which Okamoto et al. found unaffected."
- R3: frame as "consistent with large between-model differences reported by Okamoto et al. (2026) and Guzman Piedrahita et al. (2025). Here the difference is mostly in whether a family over-takes at all."
- R4: report it as a wording sensitivity that must be controlled for (cite Okamoto's discretionary wording, Bhandary et al. 2026 and PIMMUR), not as a finding.
- What we can call new: deterrence measured as a dose–response in a *repeated multi-agent commons* with a reviewer stage, explicit numeric audit odds, and each model's own measured gain, across four model families.
