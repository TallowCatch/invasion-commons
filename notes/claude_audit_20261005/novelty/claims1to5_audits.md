## How to read this note

**Depth labels** (how far I actually read each source):

- **metadata**: title, authors, venue and DOI checked (Crossref or the publisher record). Content not read.
- **abstract**: I read the abstract on arXiv, IDEAS/RePEc, a publisher page or a repository record.
- **full text (tool)**: the arXiv HTML or a PDF was opened through a fetch tool that summarises the page. The quotes are the ones the tool returned. Check them by eye before quoting them in the paper.
- **full text (direct)**: I downloaded the PDF and pulled out the text myself. The quotes are exact.
- **secondary**: the claim about the source comes from another paper that describes it. The source itself was not opened.

**Rules I followed.** Every source below was opened at the depth given. Quotes are short and exact at that depth. If I could not open something, the note says "not verified". A DOI listed without a depth label was checked against Crossref.

## 2. Verdicts, claim by claim

### Claim 1: the reviewer's goal matters more than its information (MSY target beats a one-step safety line under pressure)

- **Verdict: partly shown, in a different field.** In fisheries, stock-based targets and harvest control rules are the established answer to overharvesting under uncertainty (Reed 1979 and May et al. 1978, metadata only; Da-Rocha et al. 2016, abstract). Sutinen & Andersen 1985 (secondary) already tie enforcement outcomes to stock size relative to MSY. **No prior work found** that compares overseer objectives in an AI-oversight protocol, or that weighs goal against information.
- **Main novelty risk:** a resource economist reads this as "dynamic optimisation beats myopic rules", which is known. The "goal matters more than information" framing is the new part, and it rests on our ablation.
- **Suggested phrasing:** "In AI oversight of a shared renewable resource, the overseer's objective matters more than its information. An overseer that targets the long-run (MSY) stock outperforms one that enforces a one-step safety line wherever the resource is under pressure. This mirrors the case for stock-based harvest rules in fisheries management, here shown for oversight protocols with strategic LLM agents."

### Claim 2: a wrong model breaks an exact joint reviewer; a learning reviewer recovers; the MSY target protects against a wrong model form

- **Verdict: partly shown.** The fragility of model-based management and the need to learn are well established (Ludwig et al. 1993: "trial and error"; robust harvest rules, Da-Rocha et al. 2016). In AI control, adaptive or learning protocols exist, but they learn about the *agent*, not the *resource* (Wen et al. 2024). **No prior work found** on the specific result that a stock target makes an AI overseer robust to a wrong model form.
- **Main novelty risk:** reviewers may see it as a re-run of adaptive management. Cite Ludwig et al. and the robust-control work.
- **Suggested phrasing:** "We show that an overseer that is exact under the true resource model fails under a misspecified one, that learning the model online restores performance, and that an MSY objective limits the damage from a wrong model form. These are known concerns in resource management, quantified here for AI oversight protocols."

### Claim 3: audits help only through consequences (memory or sanctions); auditing without consequences barely reduces harm

- **Verdict: partly shown.** In economics this follows directly from Becker-style deterrence: with no fine, the expected penalty is zero. That catches matter through what follows is the logic of Harrington 1988 (leverage from records) and Landsberger & Meilijson 1982 (state-dependent penalties). Ostrom's principle 5 says the same in institutional terms. In AI control, consequences are built in, because a catch ends the game (Greenblatt et al.; Griffin et al., "safe outcome sink state"). So the AI-control literature has not tested auditing *without* consequences. Ye & Steinhardt 2026 show, for LLM agents, that reliability tracking plus escalating penalties works where simple mechanisms are exploited, but enforcement there runs on peer reports, not budgeted audits. Ivanov 2025 shows LLMs cheat even when told they are watched.
- **What is new:** a controlled breakdown, with strategic LLM agents in a dynamic commons, of audits alone against audits plus memory (allowance cuts) against audits plus fines. Also the finding that "memory" works as a sanction-free consequence.
- **Main novelty risk:** stated baldly ("audits only matter through consequences"), economists will call it obvious. Frame it as an empirical test of whether LLM agents respond to detection itself, and say it is the AI-control blind spot, since AI control assumes catch means shutdown.
- **Suggested phrasing:** "Consistent with deterrence theory and Ostrom's graduated-sanctions principle, we find that audits reduce harm by LLM agents only when a catch has consequences, either remembered (later allowances are cut, as in Harrington-style state-dependent enforcement) or fined. Detection alone barely helps. Standard AI-control games end on the first catch, so this channel has not been tested there."

### Claim 4: deterrence starts at the expected-fine break-even; predicted prospectively in 5 of 5 settings; memory plus fine halves the audit rate needed; a tighter shared cut makes cheating pay more than no audits

- **The threshold itself: already shown.** This is the classic Becker / Allingham-Sandmo condition. Sutinen & Andersen 1985 apply it to fisheries. Gans & Holden 2026 (arXiv:2609.38262) restate it for AI agents: "misconduct is deterred when its expected sanction exceeds its gain". **Do not claim the threshold.**
- **Prospective prediction from no-audit runs, 5 of 5 new settings, with LLM agents: no prior work found.** Gans & Holden is theory only and uses a single agent. I found no LLM-agent study that pre-registers or predicts the break-even and then confirms it.
- **"Memory plus fine halves the audit rate needed": partly shown.** That records-based enforcement lowers the inspections needed is Harrington's "enforcement leverage", together with Landsberger & Meilijson 1982, Friesen 2003 ("reduces inspection costs"), and Hansen, Jensen & Nøstbakken 2014 (quota resources, inspections based on track record, lower inspection cost). The factor of two is specific to our setting and is new as a measured number. Note the warning from human experiments: Clark, Friesen & Muller 2004 (secondary) found records-based schemes did no better than random audits in the lab.
- **"A tighter shared cut that allows for known cheaters makes cheating pay more than no audits": no prior work found** stating this exact result. The mechanism is the usual commons dilution: a cut shared by everyone is paid mostly by honest agents, so the cheater bears only a small share. The ambient and group-penalty literature (Segerson 1988; Xepapadeas 1991, metadata only) and team moral hazard are the nearest relatives. A search snippet claiming that cutting the total allowable catch to allow for illegal catch increases cheating was **not verified**.
- **Main novelty risk:** overclaiming the threshold. Second risk: the "halves" number reading as a general law rather than a result for our setting.
- **Suggested phrasing:** "We show that the classic expected-penalty condition (audit probability × fine > one-step gain from cheating) governs when audits deter LLM agents in a dynamic commons, and we predict the threshold prospectively from no-audit runs, confirming it in 5 of 5 new settings. In line with enforcement-leverage results (Harrington 1988), adding memory roughly halves the audit rate needed in our settings. We also identify a design trap: a uniformly tighter allowance that pre-compensates for known cheaters spreads the cost onto honest agents and makes cheating more profitable than no auditing at all."

### Claim 5: how audits are aimed matters (unpredictable, every agent reached; known schedule fails; largest-report targeting catches 25% of liars versus 100% for random; signal-aimed best in forest)

- **"A known schedule cannot deter": already shown** in inspection games (von Stengel: once inspections run out the inspectee "can and will safely use" its violations; inspector leadership means committing to a *randomised* plan). AI control shows the same thing empirically (upfront auditing beaten by rare attacks; Kutasov et al.: knowing when resampling occurs drops safety to 17%). Gans & Holden: audit draws must not be "learned in advance".
- **"Audits must reach every agent" and "largest-report targeting fixates": partly shown.** Supporting precedents:
  - the targeting scheme in Friesen 2003 that puts firms into the target group "at random";
  - Alm & McKee's summary that random audits are "necessary if the systematic audits are to be effective";
  - Ensign et al. 2018 on runaway feedback loops ("repeatedly sent back to the same neighborhoods");
  - Zavattari et al. 2026, where confidence-ranked auditing can be "worse than random" (non-strategic agents).

  **Counter-precedents that a reviewer will raise:** Reinganum & Wilde 1985 ("random audit rules are weakly dominated by audit cutoff rules") and Kamijo 2014 (lowest-reporter rule "superior to the random audit rule"). Both are static, assume commitment, and use homogeneous or one-shot settings. **No prior work found** that reports the specific failure: in a repeated, heterogeneous, multi-agent setting, a rule that audits the largest (memory-corrected) report keeps auditing the same agent, so only 25% of liars are ever caught.
- **"Aiming at a physical signal was best (forest)": partly shown in spirit.** See Ostrom 4B (monitoring the resource), Segerson's ambient-based scheme, and the finding of Fišar et al. that the selection mechanism using a noisy income signal did best. Gilpatric et al. 2011 also use "imperfect signals of compliance effort". Doing this inside an AI-oversight protocol with LLM agents is new.
- **Main novelty risk:** the tax literature says report-based targeting beats random. We must state the conditions under which ours fails: a rank rule with one audit per round, agents of different sizes, repeated play, and no random component. Otherwise reviewers will think we contradict Reinganum & Wilde.
- **Suggested phrasing:** "We confirm the inspection-game result that predictable audits cannot deter, in an LLM-agent commons. We then show a failure specific to repeated, heterogeneous settings: a rule that targets the largest memory-corrected report keeps selecting the same agent and catches only 25% of liars, against 100% under random audits. This complements static results where report-based cutoff rules beat random auditing (Reinganum & Wilde 1985). Aiming audits at a physical signal of the resource did best in the forest game, echoing Ostrom's resource-monitoring principle."
