## a) How to use this ledger

This ledger lists the sources that the R1 rerun and the S1 report-and-audit study may cite. For each source it records which identifiers were checked, how closely the source was read, and which exact terms the source uses. Before you use a literature term in the paper, check three things here: the term's row in the terminology map (section c), the "does NOT support" column of the evidence table (section b), and the cautions in section d. The depth labels mean: **full text checked** = the passages quoted here were read in the paper itself during this pass. **abstract only** = the arXiv or publisher landing page and abstract were read. **metadata only** = title, authors, year, venue and DOI were confirmed through Crossref or the publisher, but no content was read. **secondary** = the content claim comes from a reference work such as Wikipedia, not from the source. **not verified** = the details could not be confirmed. Do not cite a concept from a metadata-only or secondary row as if the source had been read. Quotes come from automated page extraction, so check the original before quoting in print.

Verification method and limits: WebSearch was not enabled for this organisation (HTTP 403). Every check used WebFetch on arXiv, the Crossref API, ScienceDirect, the University of Chicago Press, RePEc/IDEAS, Wikipedia and an author-hosted PDF. ## d) Where our framing must be careful

- **Our reviewers and actors are not LLMs, and they have no capability gap.** In AI control, "trusted" means a weaker model believed not to scheme and "untrusted" means a stronger, possibly scheming model (Greenblatt et al.). Our reviewer is a hand-coded Monte Carlo rule, and our actors are fixed policies. Say "a reviewer with a limited audit budget, by analogy with trusted monitoring". Do not say "we implement trusted monitoring".
- **Our "red team" is a parameter search.** It picks a misreporting level that is a best response to a fixed protocol. Greenblatt et al.'s red team designs attack policies, including collusion signals, against the full protocol. Call ours "a best-response misreporter" or "a one-parameter adaptive attacker", and say it is a lower bound on what an adaptive adversary could do.
- **Safety and usefulness are defined differently.** AI-control safety is the probability of *zero* catastrophes over a whole deployment, and usefulness is task correctness. Ours are per-step rates of unsafe approvals and of restriction of safe requests, plus long-run harvest. Give our metrics their own names and state the analogy, not an equivalence.
- **The "2% audit budget" is a different kind of quantity.** It is a share of tasks audited by expensive trusted labour. Our k of 6 per step is a much larger share (17–100%) with perfect verification. Results at k/6 are not comparable to the 2% regime.
- **Collusion means something narrower here.** In Greenblatt et al., collusion is coordination on a hidden signal between a policy and a monitor that are both the untrusted model. Our neighbour collusion is a scripted joint false cross-report. It does not test steganographic coordination.
- **Inspection-game results assume rational players with common-knowledge payoffs** and usually solve for an equilibrium (Avenhaus et al.). We simulate fixed or best-responding actors and do not compute equilibria. Do not claim deterrence "in equilibrium". Say "against these response rules".
- **Becker and Allingham–Sandmo were checked at metadata depth only.** Do not quote their wording or results until they are read. Their agents are expected-utility maximisers, which ours are not.
- **Varian 1990 is unverified.** Leave peer monitoring uncited or cite Greenblatt's untrusted monitoring until it is checked.
- **MSY.** rK/4 at K/2 comes from the deterministic logistic model. Under weather noise and patch spillovers, a stock-at-MSY target is a reference heuristic, not an optimum. May et al. warn that MSY policies are risky under environmental noise.
- **Chance constraint.** A Monte Carlo estimate of a 5% violation probability is a sample approximation with sampling error. Report the number of samples and the confidence, and do not call it a guaranteed 5% bound. If a union bound across patches is used, say that it is conservative under correlated weather.
- **Shielding.** Elsayed-Aly et al.'s shields are formally synthesised and give guarantees. Calling our reviewer a "shield" or "centralized shield" implies guarantees it lacks. Say "a joint reviewer, analogous to centralized shielding".
- **Novelty.** Per-instance or local monitoring that misses joint harm is already shown by Makins et al. (fragmentation effect) and Hu & Wang (observability boundary). R1/S1 can only claim the renewable-resource, stochastic-dynamics setting together with the trade-off between audit cost and restriction of safe activity.
- **Scalable oversight.** Amodei's and Bowman's definitions are about supervising systems that are expensive to evaluate or more capable than the supervisor. Our setting fits the "expensive to evaluate" sense (limited audits). It does not fit the "outperforms the supervisor" sense.

## e) Positioning

R1 and S1 copy the structure of the AI-control trusted-monitoring setup in a non-LLM sequential commons. A reviewer with limited verification capacity must approve or cut down actions, and is scored on two axes: harmful requests approved (safety) and safe activity restricted (usefulness). S1 adds the part of that setup the project currently lacks. Actors submit possibly false reports, the reviewer can verify only k of 6 per step (an audit budget), audits are either random or aimed at the most suspicious reports, peers can cross-report (similar to untrusted monitoring) and may collude, and a best-response misreporter plays the red team. The auditing side is the classic inspection-game and tax-evasion problem: an inspector with partial verification and a penalty tries to deter strategic under-reporting. We use this as background and do not solve for equilibria. Compared with Makins et al. and Hu & Wang, who already show that per-instance monitors miss distributed harm in LLM code settings, our contribution is to test the same oversight questions where harm builds up through stochastic, renewable resource dynamics. There, an unsafe joint decision is defined relative to a one-step safety line or an MSY target under a chance constraint.
