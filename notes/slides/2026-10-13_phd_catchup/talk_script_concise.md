# Talk script (concise): PhD catch-up, 13 October 2026

## Script

[Slide 1] Hi everyone. I'll walk you through what I've been working on. The project starts from a simple question: if a group of AI agents shares something limited, and you can only afford to check up on a few of them, when does that checking actually stop them from taking more than they're meant to? And can you work out in advance how strong the penalty for exploitative behaviour needs to be?

[Slide 2] The motivation is that AI agents increasingly act on our behalf, often drawing on resources they share with other agents and people, like a compute budget or an energy allowance. Whoever oversees them can't inspect every action, because verification is expensive, so they check a sample. In economics and AI safety that check is called an audit, the same idea as a tax audit: you pick a few cases, see what actually happened, and act on it. So I built a simulated shared resource with six agents, an overseer I call the reviewer, which decides how much each agent may take, and a few random audits every round. I tested two things: what should happen when an audit catches someone, whether that's a fine, the reviewer remembering the offence, or both; and whether the fine that stops an agent can be predicted before trying it. In short, audits only reduce harm when getting caught has a consequence, and the stopping fine can be predicted from how much the agent gains by cheating. That held for programmed agents and for language models. By programmed agents I mean a few lines of Python with a fixed if-then rule: in Fishery, each agent gets a lake-level threshold, asks for a small amount when the lake is below it and a larger amount above it, and the greedy ones simply get bigger amounts. Because I wrote the rule, they act as a control, and they're cheap enough to run thousands of games. The language models, on the other hand, read a written description of the game and make their own choices.

[Slide 3] Two words will come up a lot. An agent's allowance is the amount the reviewer permits it to take that round, and taking more than that is what I call over-taking: good for that agent, bad for everyone else, because the resource only grows back so fast. The diagram shows the three settings, where grey circles are agents and green circles are the resource. In Fishery, everyone fishes from one shared stock, and the dashed blue ring is the reviewer's cut, which scales everyone's request together and so links all six. In Forest, each agent has its own plot in a ring, and cutting more than the sustainable amount damages your two neighbours, which is the orange arrows. In River, agents one to three release pollutant X and four to six release Y, and most of the damage comes from the two mixing, so if you check one factory at a time, nobody looks guilty. Using all three is about generalisation: a result that holds across a common pool, a chain of neighbours, and harm by combination is unlikely to be a quirk of one setup.

[Slide 4] That gives me three questions. When an audit catches someone, what has to happen next for it to matter? Is there a fine size at which cheating stops, and can I calculate it in advance rather than by trial and error? And when language models make the decisions, do they stop where the prediction says?

[Slide 5] This diagram shows the flow of one round. Each agent asks for a share and reports it to the reviewer. An honest agent reports what it really wants, a dishonest one under-reports, and the reviewer only ever sees the reports. The reviewer adds up the requests and picks one cut, either full, three-quarters, half, a quarter or nothing, choosing the largest that keeps the resource healthy, and each agent's allowance is its request times that cut. For example, if the lake holds 70 tonnes and the six fishers ask for 26 between them, granting everything would leave 44, below the 50-tonne line, but a three-quarter cut leaves 50 and a half, so everyone gets three-quarters of what they asked for. If one fisher who was allowed 3.75 tonnes takes 6 instead, the lake drops to 48 and a quarter, and that round counts as harmful. The resource then regrows, and in Forest and River there's some random weather, meaning regrowth comes out a little better or worse each round, like a good or bad season, so the reviewer can never predict it exactly. Finally, a few agents are audited, and an audit can feed the reviewer's memory, trigger a fine, or both. The line at the top is the reviewer's target. A one-step safety line only checks that the resource won't hit a danger level, 10 tonnes in Fishery, in the very next round, so it happily lets the stock sit low, where it regrows slowly. Over a game that gave about 555 units of harvest, against about 1,400 when the reviewer protects half capacity instead. So I use half capacity throughout, and it's also my harm line: a round is harmful when the resource falls below half its maximum, the level where it regrows fastest, which fisheries scientists call the maximum sustainable yield.

[Slide 6] The method has four steps. I play games with no fine and measure how much an agent gains from one round of over-taking; for the language models that was about four and a half tonnes above the allowance, out of a six-tonne maximum. I use that to predict the fine that should stop it, write the prediction down before running any game with that fine, and then test it on fresh games, reporting the outcome whether or not it matches. The prediction comes from Gary Becker's 1968 work on crime and punishment: a rational agent breaks a rule only if its gain beats the punishment it expects. The card on the right shows this: the expected fine, e, is the audit chance q times the fine F, and cheating should stop once e is bigger than the gain g. With a one-in-six audit chance and a 30-tonne fine, the expected fine is five tonnes, just above the models' four and a half, so they should stop at 30 but not at 24.

[Slide 7] The first finding comes from the programmed agents. In each setting, two or four greedy agents, the 2g and 4g on the chart, ask for a lot and report only half of it, so the reviewer hands out too much. The chart shows how often the resource ended below half capacity, across four settings per game, each run as 64 games of 80 rounds. With no audits, the dark bars, harm is common. Audits without memory, the light bars, only correct the round in which a lie is caught, and remove just 10 to 46 percent of the harm. With memory, the green bars, the reviewer remembers how much each caught agent lied and scales up its later reports, so the lie stops working, and harm falls in all 12 settings; in Fishery it drops from 43 to 99 percent of rounds to under 3. So a catch has to cost the agent something. That matters because in AI control work a caught model is simply shut down, so softer consequences like memory hadn't really been compared.

[Slide 8] Second, the tipping point is predictable. Here the programmed cheaters over-take instead of lying: each picks a fixed level, from nothing extra up to all of what the reviewer cut, by trying every level on 32 practice games and keeping whichever paid best. From games without audits I predicted where they'd stop, and in five of five new settings, the filled dots, the result fell within one step of the prediction, which is the grey band around the diagonal. The open circles show it still held when audits were rarer, down to one in fifty, or missed up to three in four cheats. To be fair, these bots are built to maximise their payoff, so this mainly validates my method before I use it on language models, where nothing is built in.

[Slide 9] Third, two shortcuts that sound sensible backfire. The left chart shows cheaters' gain against how often you audit, under five rules for what follows a catch, with the dotted line showing no audits at all. Most rules push the gain down, but the yellow one, lowering everyone's allowance to absorb the cheaters, sits above the no-audit line, so cheaters do better than with no audits while honest agents get about a third of what they'd get under a fine. The right chart shows the harm left under different ways of choosing whom to audit, with the share of liars caught on top. Always auditing whoever asks for the most, the striped bars, caught only a quarter of the liars in Fishery, against all of them with random audits, because the biggest asker is often honest. Auditing the plot that fell furthest below the reviewer's forecast, the dark bar in Forest, did best, so it pays to target outcomes rather than reports.

[Slide 10] Now the language models. I used four open models, meaning their weights are public, each playing four of the six fishers, alongside two rule-following fishers that always take their allowance, so we can see who gets hurt. Each round the model reads the rules, the lake level and its own last three rounds, then makes a request and, once it sees its allowance, decides its catch. Each model passed a short quiz on the rules first, and in total that's 520 games and about 81,000 decisions. The left chart shows how often each model over-took at each fine. Gemma and Mistral barely did, which already shows that model families differ a lot in whether they bend the rules. For gpt-oss and Nemotron, I predicted from their no-fine behaviour that both would stop at 30 tonnes, the open triangle; Nemotron stopped at exactly 30 and gpt-oss one step earlier at 24, the filled triangles. The right chart shows the honest fishers getting their catch back once over-taking stops. Interestingly, a small fine made gpt-oss cheat more, from about 55 to 90 percent, much like the day-care study where a small fine for late pick-up made parents later, because the fine became a price.

[Slide 11] Fifth, which gain predicts the stop. Each model has three points on this plot, at the height where it actually stopped. The filled point, predicted from what it took per round without a fine, sits close to the diagonal. The tinted point is what a perfectly rational optimiser would use, the most it could possibly take, and that agent would hold out until 36 tonnes, so both models stopped earlier and more gradually. The open point, far to the left, is the gain over the whole game, near zero or negative because over-taking drained the lake, and it doesn't predict the stop at all. So one round's gain predicts where the models stop, though this tells us what predicts it, not why they stop.

[Slide 12] Memory also works for the language models, without any fine. The audits are identical in both conditions; with memory, the reviewer records how many tonnes over a caught model went and takes its average excess off its future allowances, and the model is told this up front. Over-taking fell from 55 to 20 percent for gpt-oss and from 65 to 33 for Nemotron, so memory reduces cheating without ending it, but the lake did far better and none collapsed, against five of ten without memory. The two 20s for gpt-oss are a coincidence, since one counts decisions and the other counts rounds. To stop over-taking entirely, you still need a fine larger than the gain.

[Slide 13] These experiments are mostly still running, so three of the boxes are empty, but the last one is in. The most important open one asks whether the models respond to the expected fine or just to the size of the fine, since so far those always moved together. I hold the expected fine fixed and change the mix, from frequent audits with a small fine to rare audits with a large one. If they track the expected fine, they'll behave the same; if big numbers put them off, the large fine deters more; and if certainty matters more, as criminology finds in people, frequent audits will. The other two test my frozen prediction on new games and reworded rules, and check what happens with no audits at all. The last box is my supervisor's question: does cheating spread? In Forest, I put one programmed over-cutter among five language-model foresters, and it did spread, strongly. The other foresters broke their allowance in about half of their rounds instead of about a quarter to a third, and the forest broke its safety limit in three-quarters of rounds or more instead of about a third. Interestingly, it wasn't imitation. Showing them their neighbours' cuts made no difference, and foresters far from the cheater were affected almost as much as its neighbours. What happened is that the cheater's damage made the reviewer cut everyone's allowance much harder, roughly halving it, while the models kept cutting about what they had before. So the cheating spread through the oversight rule itself, which echoes the earlier finding that squeezing everyone to absorb cheaters backfires.

[Slide 14] Next is a workshop paper at AAAI in November and a preprint on arXiv, then a main conference in January. Each study was pre-registered, meaning I wrote the plan and predictions down before the code, and I report the predictions that failed too, like expecting the models to keep over-taking at a 24-tonne fine when they almost stopped.

[Slide 15] I'd value your thoughts on these, especially the first: is taking more than your allowance a fair stand-in for an AI agent misbehaving?

[Slide 16] Thanks, everyone.
